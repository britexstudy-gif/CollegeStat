# How to write a Common App essay that sounds like you

## In short

- The essay is one small part of the application. Use it to add what grades and activities cannot show.
- Choose one real moment, not your whole life story.
- Show what you noticed and what changed in how you think. Do not announce traits such as "resilient" or "passionate".
- The seven prompts are unchanged for 2026–27. Check the live word limit in your Common App account before you finalise.

## Start from a real moment

The Common App kept the same seven essay prompts for 2026–27. [2026–27 prompts](https://www.commonapp.org/blog/announcing-2026-2027-common-app-essay-prompts/) If you started an essay in last year's account, Common App says your personal essay is kept when the new application opens. [2026–27 prompts](https://www.commonapp.org/blog/announcing-2026-2027-common-app-essay-prompts/)

Yale's admissions office puts the main point plainly: "Your perspective – the lens through which you view your topic – is far more important than the specific topic itself." It also advises picking topics that are meaningful to you and keeping your own voice. [Yale: Essays](https://admissions.yale.edu/essays)

A workable shape: a specific moment or question, what you did or thought, what changed in your understanding, and why it matters now. It is a drafting tool, not a formula.

## A weak and a stronger opening

> **Example.** A weak opening: "Ever since I was young, I have been passionate about helping people, which is why I want to study medicine."
>
> A stronger one: "My grandmother does not read the pharmacy labels in Tashkent, so every Sunday I translate them into Uzbek at her kitchen table, and every Sunday I learn which words I do not know either."

The second opening is illustrative, not a template. It works because it names a place, a task and a small gap in the writer's knowledge, which gives you something to reflect on.

## Five drafting passes

1. **List moments** that show a choice, a responsibility, curiosity, a relationship or a change. An ordinary event can work if the reflection is precise.
2. **Draft one scene** with concrete detail. Say what you noticed and decided.
3. **Add the consequence.** What do you understand differently now? What would a reader learn about how you approach people, study or problems?
4. **Cut résumé material.** Your activities list already records achievements.
5. **Read it aloud** and ask one trusted reader to mark unclear or generic lines, not to rewrite your voice.

## Choosing your prompt

The Common App gives you seven prompts, and the last one lets you write on any topic of your choice. In 2025–26, "Topic of your choice" was the most popular option, chosen by 28% of students, followed by "Facing adversity" at 23%. [2026–27 prompts](https://www.commonapp.org/blog/announcing-2026-2027-common-app-essay-prompts/) Popularity does not make a prompt better or worse for you.

In practice the prompt matters less than the story. Pick your moment first, then the prompt that fits it best. If two prompts fit, choose the one that lets you spend more words on reflection and fewer on setting the scene.

The personal essay has had a 650-word maximum for years; confirm the live limit in the writing section of your 2026–27 account. You do not need every word: a sharp, finished essay below the limit is better than a padded one at the limit.

## The essay is not the only writing

Colleges can make the personal essay required or optional, and many add their own questions or a separate writing supplement. [Common App: First-year students](https://www.commonapp.org/apply/first-year-students) These have their own limits. For 2026–27 Yale, for example, asks every first-year applicant two short-answer questions (one of them up to 200 words), and asks Common App applicants for three "short takes" of up to 200 characters each and one essay of up to 400 words chosen from three prompts. [Yale: Essay topics](https://admissions.yale.edu/essay-topics)

Plan the whole set together. If your personal essay is about family, let a supplement show your academic side. Do not tell the same story twice.

There is also an optional additional-information question. In 2025–26 Common App reduced its limit to 300 words. [Common App: What's new 2025–26 (PDF)](https://www.commonapp.org/files/Whats-New-25-26.pdf) It is for context, such as your school's grading or a disruption to your studies, not for a second essay.

## Topics that often work for students from Central Asia

You do not need a dramatic story. Some of the strongest essays are about ordinary life described precisely:

- Translating between generations or languages at home (Uzbek, Russian, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Tajik, English) and what gets lost.
- A responsibility you carry: a family business, younger siblings, a relative's care.
- A project you built because the thing you needed did not exist in your city.
- A question from a lesson, olympiad or book that you kept chasing after class.
- Moving between schools, cities or countries, and what you had to relearn.

Topics to handle carefully: a general essay about your country's history, a summary of an olympiad result already in your activities list, or a story where someone else is the hero and you only watched.

## Write in English that sounds like you

Admissions readers do not expect the English of a native speaker. They expect clear sentences and a real voice. Write the first draft in whatever language your thoughts come in fastest, then write the English version yourself. Use translation tools to check a word, never to produce paragraphs. Short sentences are fine. Idioms you would not use in conversation are not.

## A worked revision

> **Example.** A first-draft paragraph: "Volunteering at the library taught me many valuable lessons about responsibility, teamwork and leadership. I realised that I can make a difference in my community."
>
> A revised paragraph: "On my second Saturday at the district library in Bishkek, a boy asked for a chemistry book we did not have. I wrote the title on my hand. By the next week I had found a used copy, and I had also started a notebook of every book children asked for and could not get."

The revision removes the three abstract nouns and replaces them with one action the reader can see. The reflection can now follow from the notebook: what the list taught the writer, and what they did next. This is an illustration of method, not a model to copy.

## A four-week drafting plan

- **Week 1:** list ten moments in fifteen minutes a day. Pick the two you can describe in the most detail.
- **Week 2:** write a rough draft of each, without editing. Choose the one that makes you think more.
- **Week 3:** revise for reflection. Cut every sentence that only repeats your activities list.
- **Week 4:** show it to two readers, revise once, read it aloud, check the word count and paste it into a plain-text editor to remove odd formatting before pasting it into the Common App.

If you have less time, keep the order and shorten each step. Do not skip the gap between drafts; a day away from the text shows you what is unclear.

## How to use feedback

Yale suggests sharing your essays with one or two people who know you well, who can catch mistakes and suggest cuts, while you keep control of the essay. [Yale: Essays](https://admissions.yale.edu/essays)

A useful pair: one reader who knows you well (to check that the essay sounds like you) and one who does not (to check that a stranger can follow it). Give them a precise question: "Where did you lose interest?" or "What do you think I value after reading this?" If their answer is not what you meant, revise that part. Do not let anyone rewrite sentences for you; readers can tell, and the application asks you to confirm the work is yours.

## Questions to ask your reader

- After reading, what three words would you use about me?
- Which sentence would you remember tomorrow?
- Where did you have to read twice?
- Is there anything here that I already say in my activities list?
- Does the ending tell you something new, or does it repeat the opening?

## Checklist before you paste it in

- One moment, told with concrete detail.
- At least a third of the essay is reflection: what changed and why it matters.
- No sentence that could appear in anyone else's essay.
- Within the live word limit, read aloud once from start to finish.
- Consistent with your activities, recommendations and supplements.

## Mistakes to remove

- A dramatic story with almost no reflection.
- A list of accomplishments joined by transitions.
- Big claims ("I want to change the world") with no proof.
- Writing what you think an admissions officer wants instead of what happened.
- Polishing until it sounds like an adult wrote it.

## Questions students often ask

**Can I write about something sad?** Yes, if the essay spends most of its words on what you did and understood, not only on what happened to you.

**Should I mention the university I want?** Not in the personal essay, which goes to every college on your list. Use each college's own questions for that.

**Can I use an AI tool?** Check each college's policy. Even where tools are allowed for checking, the sentences and the story must be yours.

**Can I reuse my UCAS personal statement?** They are different documents. The UCAS statement is about your course; the Common App essay is about you. See [UCAS personal statement](/articles/ucas-personal-statement-2027).
