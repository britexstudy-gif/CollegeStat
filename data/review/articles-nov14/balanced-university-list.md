# How to build a balanced university list

## In short

- "Reach", "match" and "likely" are planning labels, not predictions.
- A university can be likely but unaffordable, or affordable but the wrong course. Compare courses, not just names.
- Write a source and a date next to every number you use.
- Remove any option you would not attend.

## What to record for each option

- **Entry:** required school subjects, how your certificate is treated, test policy, English score, deadline. [UCAS: before you apply](https://www.ucas.com/applying/before-you-apply)
- **Selectivity:** published admit rates or admitted-student data, if the university gives them for your student group and subject. Past results do not predict your decision. [BigFuture search](https://bigfuture.collegeboard.org/college-search)
- **Cost:** tuition, fees, housing, food, travel, insurance, personal costs. Check international fees and aid eligibility separately.
- **Academic fit:** required courses, teaching language, how easy it is to get into your major, class format, lab or internship access, progression rules.
- **Practical fit:** location, climate, travel, visa, housing, disability support, international student services.

For US colleges, College Scorecard gives university-level cost and outcomes. Read the definitions and years, because national data may not describe international students or one department. [College Scorecard](https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/)

## Build the list as a table you can update

Use a spreadsheet, one row per option. Useful columns:

1. University and exact course name.
2. Country and application system (Common App, UCAS, uni-assist, the university's own portal, a scholarship portal).
3. Deadline, with the time zone.
4. Entry rule for your certificate, with a link to the page that says it.
5. Test and English requirements.
6. Total yearly cost, and the date you checked it.
7. Aid or scholarship available to you, and its deadline.
8. Your label: reach, match, likely, financial safety.
9. Status: researching, writing, submitted, decision.

A row with an empty "entry rule" or "cost" column is not ready to be on the final list. Fill it, or remove the row.

## How many universities, and in what mix

There is no perfect number, but most students who apply abroad end up with **8 to 12 serious options**. Fewer than six leaves you exposed if one country changes its rules or one deadline slips. More than fifteen usually means weaker applications, because every university wants its own essay, documents or fee.

Some systems also set a ceiling. The Common App lets you add up to 20 colleges. [Common App: First-year students](https://www.commonapp.org/apply/first-year-students) UCAS allows up to five choices for undergraduate study, with no more than four in any one of medicine, dentistry, veterinary medicine or veterinary science, and usually only one course at either Oxford or Cambridge. [UCAS: Filling in your application](https://www.ucas.com/applying/applying-to-university/filling-in-your-ucas-application)

A mix that works for many applicants from Central Asia:

- **2–3 reach options**: the admit rate is low for everyone, or your grades and scores sit below the typical admitted student.
- **4–5 match options**: your profile is close to the middle of admitted students and you meet every stated requirement.
- **2–3 likely options**: you are clearly above the published entry rule and the course has room.
- **At least 1 financial safety**: a place you are likely to get into *and* your family can pay for without a scholarship you have not won yet.

The last line is the one students skip most often. A "likely" admission is not a safe option if, for example, the yearly cost is several times your family's budget.

## Spread the risk across countries

Different countries decide in different ways, which is useful. The US reads the whole application (essays, activities, recommendations) and offers need-based aid at some colleges. The UK decides mostly on grades, predicted results and the personal statement, through one UCAS application for up to five choices. Germany, Hungary, Italy and the Czech Republic often have low or no tuition at public universities but stricter rules about your school certificate and language. Korea, Japan, Türkiye and China run large government scholarships with their own calendars.

Putting all your options in one system means one bad outcome hits everything. Two or three countries, each with its own calendar, give you a second chance if the first round goes badly.

## Know the calendar of each system

Calendars decide what is still possible. For UK 2027 entry, the equal-consideration date for Oxford, Cambridge and most medicine, dentistry and veterinary courses was 15 October 2026 at 18:00 UK time, and the date for most other courses is 13 January 2027 at 18:00 UK time. [UCAS: Dates and deadlines](https://www.ucas.com/applying/applying-to-university/dates-and-deadlines-for-uni-applications) The UCAS application fee for 2027 entry is £34.50 for up to five choices. [UCAS: Filling in your application](https://www.ucas.com/applying/applying-to-university/filling-in-your-ucas-application)

US regular deadlines are usually in early January, and each college sets its own. Scholarship programmes in Asia and Europe often open and close earlier than university deadlines, so write their dates in a separate column.

## A month-by-month plan from now to spring

- **November:** finish the table. Check that every row has an entry rule, a cost and a deadline from an official page.
- **December:** write supplements and statements for the January deadlines. Read any early results and adjust the list.
- **January:** submit UCAS and US regular applications well before the final day. Check the dates of government scholarships you have marked.
- **February–March:** apply to systems with later deadlines (some European and Asian universities). Prepare documents that need translation.
- **April–May:** compare offers by course and total cost, not by ranking. Confirm visa and document timelines for the one you choose.

## Signals that an option is not right

- You cannot name the specific course or major you would study there.
- You have not found the international tuition fee on an official page.
- The only reason it is on the list is a ranking position.
- You would not actually go if it were your only offer.
- The application needs a test or document you cannot get in time.

Remove options like these early. A shorter list with strong applications beats a long list of rushed ones.

## A shortlisting method

1. Write your must-haves (course, language, budget ceiling, country).
2. Score each university against them and note the source and date for each number.
3. Add at least one option that works for your family budget without uncertain aid.
4. Review with your school counselor or a family member.

> **Example.** A student in Samarkand wants computer science and a family budget that cannot depend on aid. Her list has two reach choices with strong aid, three matches in different countries, two likely choices, and one low-tuition option in Germany. Each row has a note such as "tuition: official fee page, checked 10 Nov 2026". When one match turns out to require a subject she did not take, she replaces it with another match in a different country instead of adding a second reach.

## Questions to settle with your family first

- What is the most we can pay each year, in total, for four years (or three, in the UK)?
- How will money be sent abroad, and has our bank told us how long a transfer takes and what it costs?
- Which countries are we comfortable with for safety, distance and travel home?
- Do we need a degree taught fully in English, or can the student study in German, Korean or another language?
- If no aid comes, which option do we choose?

Write the answers down. They will settle many arguments in April.

## Keep the list alive

Your list is not fixed in September. Update it when you get a test score, when a university publishes new fees or deadlines, and after early-round results in December. If an early application is accepted, you may withdraw others. If it is deferred or rejected, add one or two more likely options before the January deadlines, not after.

## Mistakes to avoid

- Building the list from a ranking table and checking entry rules afterwards.
- Counting a scholarship you have not won as part of the budget.
- Applying to five versions of the same kind of university in one country.
- Forgetting that a "likely" option still needs a strong, careful application.
- Leaving costs in different currencies without converting them on the same date.

## Questions students often ask

**Is a university with a high admit rate always "likely" for me?** No. The rate may be for domestic students, or for the whole university rather than your course. Check the entry rule for your certificate first.

**Should I include a university in my own country?** Many students do, as a financial safety and a back-up if visa or document timing goes wrong. It counts as a real option if you would attend.

**How do I label a university that publishes no data?** Use its stated entry requirements and leave the label as "match" until you know more. Ask the admissions office what applicants with your certificate usually need.

## Checklist

- 8–12 options, with at least one financial safety.
- Options in at least two countries or application systems.
- Course, tuition, English requirement and deadline written down for each, with the source and date.
- Every option is somewhere you would really go.
- Reviewed with a counselor, teacher or family member.

A good list gives you real choices if the most selective ones say no. To see where each option stands, use the chances view in CollegeStat, then follow up with [need-based aid](/articles/us-need-based-aid-internationals), [UCAS 2027](/articles/ucas-2027) and [Germany routes](/articles/germany-studienkolleg) if relevant.
